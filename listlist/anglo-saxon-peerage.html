    Anglo-Saxon Peerage

    Introduction

    The concept of a peerage originates long before Anglo-Saxon England, taking varying forms but always serving to elevate certain peoples within a feudal system comprising 'classes' based on wealth, land ownership, social status, etc.

    Anglo-Saxon England comprised a number of kingdoms that rose and fell during the mediæval period, each having a local system of peerage depending on the kingdom's size, fealty to other kingdoms, etc. Generally, four groups of classes can be distinguished:

    The Monarchy — the ultimate ruling power, i.e., the king.

    The Nobility — the administrative power over a region.

    The Free Labourers — the emancipated or those born free.

    The Slaves — the indentured workers (by debt, war, or birth).

    The mediæval period evidenced the Germanic concept of a weregild; in simple terms, the monetary value attached to a person's life should they die. The implementation of weregild was complicated and varied between kings, but abstraction demonstrates a clear hierarchy. Values given here are from the Kingdom of Northumbria's Norðleoda Laga,[1] and are measured in thrymsas — a gold coin common before widespread use of silver sceats and pennies.

    In Anglo-Saxon England, a hide represented the area of land sufficient to support a household — not explicitly a measure of area, but a measure of value for tax assessment. The hide area is generally reckoned at around 120 acres,[2] and divided into four virgates/yardlands hence equivalent to around 30 acres. The acre was reckoned to represent the area of land tillable by a standard 8-oxen team in a single day.

    Monarchy

    King (cyning) — 30 000 thrymsas

    At the top was the king, a role constitutionally inherited by a male heir through a 'god-given' right to rule over the kingdom.[3] Several times during the Mediæval period, particularly bold monarchs such as Offa of Mercia and Alfred the Great of Wessex sought to expand their empires by subduing other kingly rulers through conquering or demanding fealty.

    Nobility

    Aetheling (*æþeling) — 15 000 thrymsas

    The aethelings were princes of the royal dynasty who were thus eligible for the kingship via primogeniture.[3] In time, the term was used to describe multiple members of the royal family.[4] æþele meant 'noble', and was present in the names of many Anglo-Saxon kings, perhaps most notably Æðelberht of Kent, the first Christian king in Britain.

    Gesith (ġesīþ)

    The gesith were companions to an aethel or a king, who would later travel with, advise, and defend their lords. They came to be replaced by 'thanes',[5] who are perhaps better known from Norse traditions.

    Ealdorman (ealdorman) — 8 000 thrymsas

    The ealdorman were the chief magistrates of a shire (equivalent to a modern-day county), and were in charge of leading battles and organising the collection of taxes levied by the monarch.[3]

    Reeve (ġerēfa) — 4 000 thrymsas

    The reeves were officials with localised jurisdiction under the king, and acted as the chief magistrates of a district.[4] The later office of shire-reeve (sċirġerēfa) originated the modern term of 'sheriff'.

    Thane (þæġn / þeġen / þeġn) — 2 000 thrymsas

    The thanes were aristocratic retainers (servants or attendants) of a king, to wit, a senior nobleman.[4] They were also know by the term twelfynde man (twelve-hundred man) in reference to the later weregild of 1 200 shillings. Their standard land ownership was around 5 hides (600 acres).

    Free Labourers

    Geneat (ġenēat)

    The geneat were simply retainers, to wit, a fully-free member of the peasant aristocracy.[3] They held some savings and lived on land rented or purchased from local nobility.

    Churl (ċeorl) — 200 thrymsas

    The churls were free peasants.[3] They were also known by the term twyhunde man (two-hundred man) in reference to their weregild. In most cases, churls were defined by the simple fact that they were free men but poorer than nobility.

    Gebur (ġebūr)

    The geburs were simple land owners or farmers.[3] Their standard land ownership was around 1/4 of a hide (30 acres).

    Cotsetla (cotsetla / cotsǽtla)

    The cotsetla occupied the lowest 'rung' of the Anglo-Saxon labourers, as they were cottagers or free-labourers who had been emancipated (i.e., freed from indentured work).[3] Their standard land ownership was around a mere 5 acres, and was either rented, or purchased but with the expectation of regular provision of farmed goods to the landlord.

    Slaves

    Theow / Thrall (þēow)

    The theow or thralls were indentured workers or slaves.[6] A person could be born a thrall or become indentured through capture / payment for unpaid debts. This was effectively the lowest status one could have as a citizen of Anglo-Saxon and Mediæval society, above only outlawry (wherein the protections of the law no longer protected you, and you could be killed or worse without facing repercussion).
