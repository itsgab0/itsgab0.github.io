    Quote Glosses and Translations

    Summary

    For the purposes of this website, I've performed as many translations by myself as I have been willing to attempt. I've studied how old languages like Old English, Latin (Classical), and Ancient Greek (Attic) were written in their respective time periods, and have used various digital resources to source vocabulary and grammar guides. Therefore, I take full blame and responsibility for any errors present in the texts within the 'Quotes' section. Below, I outline a few specific translation cases.

    "If I am ..." — Joan of Arc

    Joan of Arc (ca. 1431 ce)

    Firstly, in Mediæval French the quoted was known variously as Jeanne d'Ay de Domremy (by King Charles VII, ca. 1429 ce), la Pucelle (the maiden), or just Jehanne (self-reference in letters by dictation); also Jeanneton Darc ('birth name' according to Encyclopædia Britannica, ca. 1911 ce). The general consensus for her Modern English name is Joan of Arc.

    The quote comes from Joan of Arc, as she was taken into custody by the British-aligned Bishop of Beauvais, Pierre Cauchon; he was to interrogate her and find evidence of crimes punishable by excommunication and death — as she was proving too powerful an asset for the Dauphin-aligned armies, and had claimed she had been asked by God to defeat the British and return France to French rule. The bishop was a theologian and philosopher as well as an ordained priest, and so aimed to trick Joan of Arc via a scholarly trap.

    The recorded transcript describes the bishop asking her if she believed she was in God's grace — if she were to answer 'yes', she would be claiming certainty of the unknowable (according to church doctrine) God, and would be punished for heresy; however if she were to answer 'no', she would be admitting she was not sent by God, and her reputation would be ruined.

    The quote itself is recorded in many variations, but the original words would have been in Middle French (FRM), possibly even in a Norman-like dialect; although the language of European scribes at the time was Mediæval Latin, which is how many versions are transmitted.

    Variations

    LATIN

    Respondit quod "Si ipsa non sit, Deus ponat; et si ipsa sit, Deus eam teneat."

    [She answered that "If she was not, may God put her there; and if she was, may God keep her there."]

    — Guillaume Manchon, writing of Joan of Arc (ca. 1431).[1]


    Respondit: "Si ego sim, Deus me teneat; si ego non sim, Deus me velit ponere."

    [She answered: "If I am, may God keep me there; if I am not, may God put me there."]

    — Guillaume Colles (aka Boysguillaume), speaking of Joan of Arc (ca. 1455).[1]


    Respondit: "Si ego non sim, Deus ponat me; si ego sim, Deus me teneat in illa."

    [She answered: "If I am not, may God put me there; if I am, may God keep me there."]

    — Pierre Tisset writing of Joan of Arc (1960).[2]


    MIDDLE FRENCH

    Se je ny suis, Dieu m'y veuille mettres; et se je y suis, Dieu m'y veuille tenir.

    [If I am not, may God want to put me there; and if I am, may God want to kepe me there.]

    — Joan of Arc (ca. 1431).[3]


    MODERN FRENCH

    Si je n'y suis, Dieu veuille m'y mettre; si j'y suis, Dieu veuille m'y tenir.

    [If I am not, may God want to put me there; if I am, may God want to keep me there.]

    — Joan of Arc (ca. 1431).[4]


    "Lacrimosa ..." — Dies Irae

    'Dies irae' (ca. 10th cent. ce), verses 18 – 19

    The Dies irae is a Latin hymn originating from the 13th century and mostly represented in the Catholic tradition during the Last Rites. The 'Lacrimosa' section of the chant/hymn tells of Requiem at the end of all things. In modern times (as I can't speak for the olden days), the most famous rendition is that by none other than Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart.
    The below is taken from a 1962 Roman Missal (as well as Mozart's version).

    Lacrimosa dies illa
    Qua resurget ex favilla
    Judicandus homo reus:
    Huic ergo parce, Deus:
    Pie Jesu Domine
    Dona eis requiem.

    My Translation

    Full of tears, that day will be,
    When from the ash again will rise,
    To be judged, the guilty's plea;
    Spare him God, may you baptise;
    Holy Jesus, Father, Lord,
    May requiem be their reward.

    My Literal Translation

    Full of tears (will be) that day
    When will rise again from (the) ash
    To be judged the guilty man;
    This (man) ergo spare, O God;
    Holy Jesus Lord,
    Grant this (man) requiem.

    Other Translation(s)

    Irons, W. J. (1849): "Ah! that day of tears and mourning, From the dust of earth returning Man for judgement must prepare him,Spare, O God, in mercy spare him. Lord, all-pitying, Jesus blest, Grant them Thine eternal rest."

    much more accurately replicates the metre of the original but I feel the wording is a bit overly verbose and is a less precise correspondence

    "So the shaper of men ..." — The Wanderer

    'The Wanderer' (ca. 13th cent. ce), lines 85 – 87

    'The Wanderer' was a poem originally written in the 10th century, in an archaic form of English imaginatively named 'Old English'. This form of the English language predates most French and Latin influence, and far more closely resembled the West Germanic languages it was closely related to. The author(s) and compiler(s) are still unknown. These few lines of the poem stuck out to me as a classic testament to man's hubris (but not in a poncy way I swear I just think it's a cool analogy for people who try desperately to be remembered in any meaningful way and fail, like making a website with all your ideas and bits of writing and—).

    Original Style

    Yþꝺe ꞅƿa þiꞅne eaꞃꝺᵹeaꞃꝺ ælꝺa ꞅcyppenꝺ
    oþþæꞇ buꞃᵹƿaꞃa bꞃeahꞇma leaꞅe
    ealꝺ enꞇa ᵹeƿeoꞃc iꝺlu ꞅꞇoꝺon.

    Modern Style

    Ȳþde swā þisne eardġeard ælda sċyppend
    oþþæt burgwara breahtma lēase
    eald enta ġeweorc īdlu stōdon.[5]

    My Gloss

    (laid to waste) (so) (this) (dwelling-place) (of men) (shaper)
    (until) (of citizens) (of noise) (devoid)
    (ancient) (of giants) (work) (empty) (they stood)

    My Translation

    So the shaper of men laid this dwelling to waste
    until devoid of the clamour of citizens
    the ancient works of giants stood bereft.


    "For indeed there is nothing ..." — Iliad

    Zeus — Homer 'Iliad' (ca. 8th cent. bce, 17.446 – 447.

    This quote from Homer's Iliad is originally written in Homeric Greek (GRC). The quote is said by Zeus, King of the Gods in ancient Greek mythology and religion. I found myself personally unsatisfied by the available translations as I thought they either added or removed important aspects of the quote; or they used language I found too flowery or too bland. Of course, in my opinion I strike a balance that best suits my own sensibilities but I have no doubt many would find my own translation unsatisfactory — and that's fine. Just please don't tell me or it might hurt my self-esteem.

    Οὐ μὲν γάρ τί πού ἐστιν ὀϊζυρώτερον ἀνδρὸς
    πάντων, ὅσσά τε γαῖαν ἔπι πνείει τε καὶ ἕρπει.[6]

    Gloss

    (ū mèn gár – for not, no indeed), (ū [...] tí pū́ – nobody/nothing anywhere [some(one/thing).SG.N.NOM])
    (estin – it is, there is [be.3SG.PRES.ACT.IND])
    (oizdyrṓteron – more woeful/pitiful/miserable [woeful.3SG.N.CMP])
    (andròs – of/than man [man.SG.GEN])
    (pántōn – of all [all.PL.GEN.(M/N)])
    (hóssá – as (far as))
    (te gáìan épi – over the/on(to) earth [earth.POETIC.SG.ACC])
    (pnẹ̄́ẹ̄ te kaì hérpẹ̄ – breathes and crawls)

    My Translation

    For indeed there is nothing more woeful than man,
    as far as all that breathes and crawls on the Earth.

    Other Translation

    Murray, A.T. (1924): "For in sooth, there is naught, I ween, more miserable than man among all things that breathe and move upon earth"

    poetic but very lengthy, and unsemantic additions

    Kline, A. S. (2009): "For what is there more miserable than man, among all things that move and breathe on earth?"

    accurate, but personally not a fan of rendering 'ou men gár' as a question, and I think 'among' detracts from the elaborate construction of 'pántōn hóssá [...] épi'.

    Lattimore, R.: "Among all creatures that breathe on earth and crawl on it there is not anywhere a thing more dismal than man is"

    I dislike the reversed structure that feels like an English teacher insisting clause order must be one way, then finishes on a verb; although 'there is not anywhere' is a very accurate rendering of 'ū [...] tí pū́ '

    Fagle, R.: "There is nothing alive more agonized than man of all that breathe and crawl across the earth"

    mostly ignores 'ū mèn gár' for some reason, and in English, 'agonised' carries a different nuance to 'woeful'/'miserable' that other translations use — one of pain or torture as opposed to dejection or a sullen mood
