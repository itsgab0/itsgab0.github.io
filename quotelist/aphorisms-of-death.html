    Aphorisms of Death

    17th cent. ce

    " Et in Arcadia ego. "

    Also in Paradise, I am [there].

    — Guercino 'Et in Arcadia ego', (ca. 1618)[1]

    15th cent. ce

    " Sic trānsit glōria mundī. "

    Thus passes the earthly glory.

    — Catholic papal coronation rituals (ca. 1409 – 1963)[2]

    Cf. "O quam cito transit gloria mundi" — [How quickly the glory of the world passes away], as used by Thomas à Kempis in 'The Imitation of Christ' (1418).[3]

    13th cent. ce

    " Michi nīl sapientia prōdest mē reddit fatuum mors sēva. "

    Wisdom is of no use to me [when] cruel Death makes a fool of me.

    — Anonymous author (ca. 13th cent.) of agglomerated 'Lamentatio morituri' (ca. 14th cent.)[4]


    " This too shall pass "

    " این نیز بگذرد  "

    [ īn nēz bugzarad ]

    — (English) William Blackwood 'The Revolutions in Europe' (1848)[5]
    — (Persian) (probably) Attar of Nishapur (ca. 1145 – 1221)[6]


    " Morz, toi suelent cremir li sage : or queurt chascuns a son damage. "

    O Death, the wise always feared you; but [now], all rush to their own doom.

    — Helinand of Froidment 'Vers da la Mort' (ca. 1200)[4]

    12th cent. ce

    " Contrā vim mortis nōn est medicāmen in hortīs. "

    There is no medicine in the garden against the power of death.

    — (probably) Johannes de Mediolano (ca. 1100) via commentary by Arnaldus de Villanova (ca. 13th cent.) 'Regimen sanitatis Salernitatum' (ca. 1500)[7]

    Commonly quoted as "Contrā vim mortis nōn crēscit herba [var. salvia] in hortīs" — [The garden grows no herb [var. sage — dual-meaning of 'wise man'] against the power of earth].[8]

    2nd cent. ce

    " Hominem tē esse mementō. "

    Remember you are a man.

    — Tertullian 'Apologeticus' (ca. 2nd cent.)[9]


    " Mementō morī. "

    Remember to die.

    — Anonymous author (bef. 2nd cent.)[2]

    1st cent. ce

    " In ictū oculī. "

    " ἐν ῥιπῇ ὀφθαλμοῦ "

    [ en ṛipḗì op̣ṭalmû ]
    In the blink of an eye.

    — (Latin) Anonymous Pelagian author in 'Vetus Latina' (ca. 400)[10]
    — (Koine Greek) Paul the Apostle 'First Epistle to the Corinthians' (ca. 53 – 54)[11]


    " Omnia mūtantur, nihil interit. "

    Everything changes, nothing perishes.

    — Ovid 'Metamorphoses' (ca. 8 ce)[12]


    " Vulnerant omnēs, ultima necat. "

    Every [hour] wounds, the final kills.

    — (probably) Seneca the Elder (ca. 54 bce – 39 ce)[12]

    1st cent. bce

    " Stat sua cuīque diēs. "

    For each, their day is set.

    — Virgil 'Aeneid' (ca. 19 bce)[13]


    " Time flies. "

    " Fugit irrepārabile tempus. "

    It escapes, irretrievable time.

    — Virgil 'Georgics' (ca. 29 bce)[14]


    " Pulvis et umbra sumus. "

    We are dust and shadow.

    — Horace 'Odes' (ca. 1st cent. bce)

    2nd cent. bce

    " Ubi sunt? "

    " ποῦ εἰσιν "

    [ pû eisin ]
    Where are they[now].

    — (Latin) Anonymous author in 'Vetus Latina' (ca. 4th cent.)[10]
    — (Koine Greek) Anonymous author 'Book of Baruch' (ca. 2nd cent. bce)

    5th cent. bce

    " Ars longa, vīta brevis. "

    " ὁ βίος βραχύς, ἡ δὲ τέχνη μακρή "

    [ ho bíos braḳús, hē dè téḳnē makrḗ ]
    Life is short, [acquiring a] craft is long.

    — (Latin) Seneca the Younger 'De Brevitate Vitae' (ca. 1st cent.)
    — (Ancient Greek) Hippocrates 'Aphorismi' (ca. 5th cent. bce)

    The commonly used Latin phrase has reversed the order of the Ancient Greek text, and reads '[Acquiring a] craft is long, life is short.


    " Tempus edax rerum. "

    " πανθ᾽ ὁ μέγας χρόνος μαραίνει "

    [ panṭ' ho mégas ḳrónos maraínẹ̄ ]
    LAT: O Time, devourer of all.
    GRC: Mighty Time wears away all things.

    — (Latin) Ovid 'Metamorphoses' (ca. 8 ce)
    — (Ancient Greek) Sophocles 'Ajax' (ca. 5th cent. bce)[15]

    Cf. "τὸ τέλος ὁ χρόνος ἀπαιτεῖ" — [Time demands an ending], as used by (probably) Seikilos in the 'Seikilos epitaph' (ca. 1st – 2nd cent. bce).[16]
    Also, cf. "τὰ δ᾽ ἄλλα συγχεῖ πάνθ᾽ ὁ μαγκρατὴς χρόνος" — [All-powerful Time brings all else into chaos], as used by Sophocles in 'Oedipus at Colonus' (ca. 400 bce).[17]
    Also, cf. "χρόνος δ᾽ ἀμαυροῖ πάντα κεἰς λήθην ἄγει" — [Time obscures all and leads them into oblivion], as used by Sophocles (ca. 5th cent. bce) in an unnamed play.[17]
    Also, cf. "ἅπανθ᾽ ὁ μακρὸς κάναρίθμητος χρόνος φύει τ᾽ ἄδηλα καὶ φανέντα κρύπτεται" — [Enduring and immeasurable Time brings forth all that is obscured just as he conceals all that has been revealed], as used by Sophocles in 'Ajax' (ca. 5th cent. bce).[18]


    " For dust thou art, and unto dust thou shalt return "

    " Quia pulvis es et in pulverem reverteris. "

    " ὅτι γῆ εἶ καὶ εἰς γῆν ἀπελεύσῃ "

    " וְאֶל־עָפָ֖רתָּשֽׁוּב､כִּֽי־עָפָ֣ר אַ֔תָּה "

    GRC: [ hóti gê éì kaì ẹ̄s gên apeleúsēi ]
    HBO: [ kî-ʿāpār ʾattâwǝʾel-ʿāpārtāšûb ]

    — (English) English College, Douai 'Douay-Rheims Bible' (ca. 1600)
    — (Koine Greek) Anonymous Ptolemaic author (ca. 3rd cent. bce) in the 'Septuagint'
    — (Biblical Hebrew) Jahwist source (ca. 5th cent. bce) in 'Genesis'

    6th cent. bce

    " Everything flows. "

    " πάντα ῥεῖ "

    [ pánta ṛệ ]

    — Heraclitus (ca. 6th – 5th cent. bce)


    " All flesh is grass "

    " πᾶσα σὰρξ ὡς χόρτος "

    " כָּל־הַבָּשָׂ֣ר חָצִ֔יר "

    GRC: [ pâsa sàrks hōs ḳórtos ]
    HBO: [ kol-habbāśār ḥāṣîr ]

    — (Koine Greek) (probably) Saint Peter 'First Epistle of Peter' (ca. 1st cent.)
    — (Biblical Hebrew) Anonymous author (ca. 6th cent. bce) in the 'Book of Isaiah'

    Footnote

    Texts in Latin (Classical, Late, Mediæval), Greek (Ancient [Attic], Koine), and Hebrew (Biblical) are rendered using modern conventions to aid readability.

    • Latin text is rendered in mixed case with word spacing and macrons marking long vowels. Epigraphic Latin typical of ca. 1st cent. ce was rendered in majuscule (upper-case), with interpuncts (·) marking word boundaries, apices marking long 'a, e, o, u', and i longa (ꟾ) marking long 'i'.

    • Greek text is rendered in mixed case with word spacing, breathing marks, pitch accent diacritics, and iota subscript (ypogegrammeni). Epigraphic Greek typical of ca. 5th cent. bce was rendered in majuscule, scripta continua (no word spacing), and with no diacritics.

    • Hebrew text is rendered in Hebrew 'square' script with vowels marked with niqqud from the Tiberian vocalisation system. Early Biblical texts were rendered in the Palaeo-Hebrew script, while later texts were rendered in the Aramaic script – both were abjads that only represented consonants (except consonants used as 'mater lectiones' to represent some vowels).
